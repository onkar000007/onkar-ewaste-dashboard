WHY NO CREDIT-BY-CREDIT BREAKDOWN IS SHOWN FOR THESE 10 BUILDINGS
1. Version mismatch: all ten buildings were certified under LEED-NC v2.0, LEED India, or LEED O+M/EBOM — not LEED v5 (2026), whose category structure is shown in Section 1. Category weights and maximum points differ substantially between versions (v2.0 topped out at 69 points; v5 tops out at 110), so mapping old scores onto the v5 rubric would mean fabricating numbers.
2. Disclosure limits: GBCI publicly releases only the overall point total and certification tier for most projects. Wipro and Suzlon are the two exceptions where a total score (57 pts) is public — but even for these two, no subtopic breakdown exists in the public record.

3. Vidyalankar Institute Building — Independent Sustainability Self-Assessment

WHAT THIS SECTION IS — AND IS NOT
The Vidyalankar Institute building has NOT been submitted for, registered under, or awarded any LEED certification. No GBCI scorecard exists. Everything below is a self-generated estimate, scored directly against the official Section 1 category weights, built only from publicly documented architectural design features (site layout, envelope design, orientation) and honest assumptions about what is typically left undocumented in Indian institutional buildings.
Where no data exists, this assessment scores LOW — deliberately — rather than assuming credit the building has not demonstrated. Silence in the public record is treated as a gap, not as a hidden strength.

Methodology: category maximums below are taken directly from the official LEED v5 BD+C New Construction column in Section 1 (Integrative Process 1, Location & Transportation 15, Sustainable Sites 11, Water Efficiency 9, Energy & Atmosphere 33, Materials & Resources 18, Indoor Environmental Quality 13, Project Priorities 10 = 110 total). Each category is then self-scored 0 to max based on documented evidence only.

3.1 Actual Self-Assessed Rating: 30 / 110 points (≈ 27%)

ESTIMATED GRADE: F (Below Certified threshold) — ≈ 27%
Using the official LEED thresholds (Certified 40–49 pts, Silver 50–59, Gold 60–79, Platinum 80+ on this 110-point scale), this building's self-assessed score falls well short of even the base Certified tier.
This is not a criticism of the architecture — the passive daylighting and ventilation strategy is a genuine, well-considered design move, and it is the one place this assessment gives real credit. It is a statement about documentation and verification: without published data on water systems, energy performance, and materials sourcing — three of the four heaviest-weighted categories in LEED v5 — a building cannot be credibly scored above 'unproven', no matter how good its design intent looks in photographs and press descriptions.

3.2 Brutally Honest Summary

  • Strongest area: Indoor Environmental Quality (7/13) — the natural ventilation and daylighting strategy is real, deliberate, and the only category with a credible, evidence-backed design narrative behind it.
  • Weakest area: Water Efficiency (1/9) and Project Priorities (0/10) — the former has zero public data, the latter is a hard zero because the building was never registered with GBCI at all.
  • Biggest missed opportunity: Energy & Atmosphere is the single largest category in LEED v5 (33 of 110 points) and remains almost entirely undocumented — no EPI, no metering, no renewables, no commissioning report. This one gap alone accounts for roughly a third of the building's total point shortfall.
  • Bottom line: good architectural intent is not the same as a verified green building. Until Vidyalankar publishes or commissions actual performance data (energy audits, water audits, materials documentation) and formally registers with GBCI, any 'rating' — including this one — remains an estimate, not a certification.

4. Conclusion

India's green building movement, as evidenced by the official LEED v5 scorecard structure in Section 1 and the ten verified case studies in Section 2, is neither recent nor marginal — it dates back to 2003 (CII-Godrej GBC, the first Platinum building outside the United States) and has produced repeat, credible achievements across two decades: the world's largest Platinum-rated building at the time (Wipro, 2005), India's first corporate Platinum building (ITC Green Centre, 2004), the largest Platinum-rated hotel globally (ITC Grand Chola, 2012), and even a 1977-built heritage hotel recertified to Platinum under LEED O+M (ITC Maurya). This is a genuine, internationally significant track record, not isolated PR moments — and it establishes a very high, well-documented bar for what 'green building excellence' actually looks like in the Indian context: independently verified performance data, third-party audited scorecards, and public certification records that survive scrutiny.

The Vidyalankar Institute building, assessed in Section 3 against the real LEED v5 category weights from Section 1, does not clear that bar — and the honest, self-assessed estimate of 30/110 (≈ 27%, grade F (Below Certified threshold)) reflects that directly. This is not a failure of imagination or design intent: the horizontal, low-rise layout and the perforated/polycarbonate skin genuinely represent thoughtful passive design thinking around daylight and natural ventilation, and that shows up as the one credible strength in the assessment (Indoor Environmental Quality, 7 of 13 points). But intent is not evidence. Water Efficiency, Energy & Atmosphere, and Materials & Resources — together worth 60 of 110 possible points, more than half the entire v5 scorecard — have essentially no public data behind them, and Project Priorities is a hard zero because the building has never been registered with GBCI. A building cannot be rated as sustainable on the strength of what it might be doing; it has to be rated on what it can prove.

The core lesson this comparison surfaces is a distinction worth carrying forward: the difference between a green building and a green-looking building is verification, not vocabulary. Every one of the ten Section 2 case studies earned its status through registration, documentation, third-party auditing, and a public scorecard — some of them over two decades before LEED v5 even existed. Vidyalankar, or any institution in its position, has a clear and achievable path to close this gap: commission an energy audit and publish the EPI, document water fixture specifications and any rainwater harvesting infrastructure, disclose materials sourcing and recycled content data, and formally register the project with GBCI rather than relying on architectural description alone. Until that happens, the responsible position — and the one this document takes — is to treat the Vidyalankar building's sustainability credentials as promising but unproven, and to resist the temptation, however tempting for a project submission, to dress an estimate up as a certification. Honest scorecards, even unflattering ones, are worth more than borrowed authority.
